## Literature and Theoretical Background

The fundamental theory when considering monetary economics would be the impossible trinity. The trinity is derived from the Mundell-Fleming model and relies on a few conditions; namely: an open economy (no autarky), an active monetary authority and financial instruments across borders must be seen as close substitutes, incentivising investors to chase higher interest rates across borders.

The trinity states that a country must choose 2 of these 3 conditions with respect to their monetary policy. Firstly, free capital mobility, meaning capital can flow in and out of the country freely. Secondly, Monetary autonomy, meaning the central bank can set interest rates to target specific policy (inflation and output goals). Finally, exchange rate management, meaning the currency is pegged against or tied to other currencies.

![The policy trilemma: free capital mobility, exchange-rate management and monetary autonomy; pick one side of the triangle](/images/trilemma.png)

An example of this would be if you pegged your currency and had free capital mobility (side A). If you lowered your interest rates below your pegged countries interest rates, investors would flee overseas for higher returns. The selling of your currency to buy foreign currency, would put downward pressure on your currency. To maintain the peg, the central bank would have to use foreign reserves to purchase its own currency, and a lower quantity of liquid money in the economy, would push up interest rates back to the same level as the foreign country. Therefore, you’ve lost your monetary independence.

This theory allows us to “classify” the central banks of ASEAN+3. Firstly, I need to check if the assumptions of the Mundell-Fleming model are realistic in the ASEAN+3 economies. Generally, the 3 assumptions are quite fitting. No economies in ASEAN+3 are closed economies. Financial instruments are mostly closely related across borders, and every country has an active monetary authority.

However, this classification does not mean we can assume performance of central banks with similar policy stances will be similar. Central banks can position themselves at one corner and then act differently to gain the benefits of contracts formed based on future expectations of the position.

Conventional monetary policy conjects that the central bank can influence money supply in the economy through interest rates. However, this assumes that fiscal policy, set by the government, balances itself to stay solvent. The alternative perspective of this is the fiscal theory of the price level (FTPL).

Generally, the real value of government debt must equal the present value of expected future cash surpluses. This can be done in 2 ways: Firstly, through adjusting the fiscal policy to reduce outward spend or increase revenue (tax), or the general price level must increase to balance the deficit (inflation).

This creates 4 possible outcomes of the interaction between fiscal and monetary policy, according to FTPL

|                  | Fiscal Passive                     | Fiscal Active                                                         |
|------------------|------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Monetary active  | Standard central bank independence | Explosive debt, no stable equilibrium                                 |
| Monetary passive | Indeterminate                      | Fiscal dominance, where FTPL applies and fiscal policy anchors prices |

This leads to 2 stable and dominant perspectives, one of an active fiscal policy and passive monetary policy, where FTPL applies, and the other, where Monetary policy is active with a passive fiscal policy. This leads to the standard monetary policy theory that is mainstream.

## Narrowing the scope: Singapore, China and Japan

Focusing on 3 specific countries: Singapore, China and Japan; allows us to discuss in more detail the methods in which their central banks function to acheive the policy aims they set out. All 3 have mandates that target price stability with China and Singapore directly targeting sustainable growth. Additionally, all 3 target financial-sector stability through supervision of the financial sector.

Additionally, one of the largest disparities between the 3 central banks is the instrument they use to achieve their mandate. Japan uses short-term policy rate (plus balance sheet management). China uses short and long-term policy rates and window guidance from the central government. Singapore utilises the slope, midpoint and width of its exchange rate targeting (S\$NEER) to manage prices.

Overall, the comparison of the 3 countries looks like this

|                 | **Japan**                              | **China**                                            | **Singapore**                              |
|-----------------|----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Main instrument | Short-term policy rate , balance sheet | Multiple rates and window guidance                   | S\$NEER slope, midpoint, width             |
| Exchange rate   | Free float                             | Managed float, daily fixing                          | Managed basket band, undisclosed           |
| Capital account | Open                                   | Controlled, gradually liberalising                   | Open                                       |
| Fiscal angle    | Very high debt                         | Local government and LGFV debt, financial repression | Surpluses and reserves backing credibility |
